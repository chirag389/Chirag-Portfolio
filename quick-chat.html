01

Overview

Quick Chat is the customer communication product within Quick Hub, a marketing automation ecosystem. When I joined the project, Quick Chat was primarily a chatbot creation tool where businesses could create, customize, and deploy bots to handle customer conversations. I worked on expanding the product beyond chatbot creation into a broader communication platform, covering chatbot improvements, human handoff, WhatsApp communication, templates, broadcast, and workflow automation.

Role

Product Designer

Scope

Discovery → IA → Flows → UI → Prototype → Handoff

Platform

B2B SaaS · Desktop

02

The problem

The chatbot could start the conversation. It couldn't manage what happened next.

Quick Chat could automate common customer questions, but some conversations needed human help, another channel, or follow-up later.

01

The chatbot couldn't handle everything

The chatbot could answer common questions, but some customers still needed more help or a person to continue the conversation.

02

Conversations moved between channels

When customers needed more help, conversations continued through different channels, making them harder to manage in one place.

03

Previous context was harder to carry forward

When a conversation continued elsewhere, the next person had less context about what had already happened.

04

Follow-up work was still manual

Teams had to handle many follow-ups themselves, repeating the same actions for different customers.

These gaps raised a bigger question: what should happen after the chatbot?

What I found

Four discoveries changed what Quick Chat needed to become.

01

The bot needed business context

Businesses needed more than a generic chatbot. They needed to give the bot their own product knowledge, FAQs, and instructions so it could respond in a way that matched their business.

02

Some conversations needed a human

When the bot could not resolve a conversation, the customer still needed a way to reach a person without losing the context of what had already been discussed.

03

WhatsApp was the natural next channel

Small and medium businesses use WhatsApp as a direct way to communicate with their customers. While researching how to support that behaviour, I looked into the WhatsApp Business Platform and the requirements around business messaging. That research showed that the experience needed to account for platform-specific messaging constraints.

04

The work didn't stop at the conversation

Looking beyond the conversation exposed another problem: teams still had to handle follow-ups, updates, tags, and other next steps manually after a customer interaction. That revealed an opportunity to automate what happens before, during, and after a conversation.

04

The product decision

Quick Chat needed to become more than a chatbot.

Instead of continuing to add isolated features to the chatbot, the product needed to support the broader customer communication journey. That created three product directions, and each one followed from a problem the previous one exposed.

Designing around WhatsApp's 24-hour messaging window

Platform Constraint

WhatsApp allows free-form messaging only inside a 24-hour customer service window. After it closes, an approved template is required to reconnect.

UX Implication

The composer cannot behave the same way in both states, and the agent should not have to know the rule to work correctly.

Design Response

The composer changes with the session state and guides the agent toward an approved template when the window is closed.

01
The conversation window expires

Once the 24-hour window closes, free-form messaging is no longer available.

02
Choose an approved message

The agent can select an approved template instead of composing a new message.

03
Personalize before sending

Variables can be updated and the final message previewed before it is sent.

04
Add media and check the preview

Filled variables and attached media render in a live WhatsApp preview before sending.

05
The template lands in the same thread

The personalized message is delivered in the existing conversation and recorded in the customer journey.

07.3

Design review & iteration

The first structure was too channel-specific

This work was reviewed internally rather than through formal usability testing, so the change below is a design decision made in review, not a tested result.

Design Review
WhatsApp started as its own product area

The first structure placed WhatsApp beside the chatbot as a separate top-level area, mirroring how the integration works technically.

Issue Identified
The same objects appeared twice

Conversations, customer context and handoff existed in both areas, which meant an agent would learn two versions of the same task depending on the channel.

Change
Channels moved inside the chatbot

Webchat and WhatsApp became channels within the chatbot area, sharing one conversation and customer-context model, with only the channel-specific parts kept separate.

Resulting Design
One conversation model, two channels

Templates and broadcast stay inside WhatsApp because the platform requires them, while everything shared lives in one place. Workflow automation remained a separate branch.

08

Outcome

The unit of the product changed from a bot to a conversation.

Before this work, everything in Quick Chat was organised around the bot: you configured it, deployed it, and the product's job ended there. The structure I shipped is organised around the conversation instead, with the bot, the agent, the channel and the automation all acting on the same object. That is what made handoff, WhatsApp and workflows additions to one model rather than three new products.

I did not have post-launch usage data for this work, so I can only speak to what changed in the product and why, not to what it moved in the business.

What Was True After That the Product Couldn't Do Before
01
A conversation could change hands

Bot to agent, agent to agent, without the customer repeating themselves.

02
A conversation could change channel

Webchat and WhatsApp share one inbox and one customer record.

03
Platform rules became visible work

The 24-hour window and template approval appear where they block the agent, not in documentation.

04
Follow-up became configurable

Work that ran after a conversation could be defined once instead of repeated per customer.

Three Decisions That Shaped It
Decision

Treat WhatsApp as a channel, not a product area.

Why

Mirroring the integration would have duplicated conversations, context and handoff, and made agents learn the same task twice.

Result

One conversation model with channel-specific parts isolated. Adding a channel later is an extension, not a rebuild.

Decision

Surface messaging limits inside the composer.

Why

The 24-hour window is a rule agents cannot negotiate. Explaining it after a failed send would have made the product look broken instead of constrained.

Result

The expired window offers approved templates as the next step, so the constraint arrives with a way through it.

Decision

Stage bot and workflow creation instead of exposing one configuration surface.

Why

Knowledge, behaviour, testing and deployment are separate decisions with different stakes. Collapsing them hides which one you are getting wrong.

Result

A sequence with a sandbox before deploy, and every step still editable after launch. The trade-off is more steps up front.

09

What I learned

I joined this project expecting to design features. Most of the work turned out to be deciding where each feature belonged, and what the product would have to become to hold it.

01
Complexity should be staged, not hidden.

My first instinct with bot creation was to reduce the number of steps. That was the wrong goal: the decisions were genuinely separate, and compressing them only made it harder to tell which one had gone wrong. Sequencing them cost more clicks and bought clarity, and I reused the same reasoning when workflow creation raised the same question.

Bot Details Knowledge Behaviour Test Deploy
02
Platform constraints are product decisions.

WhatsApp's rules were handed to me as technical requirements. Reading the platform documentation myself changed how I designed: the 24-hour window and template approval are not edge cases, they are the shape of the workflow. Once I designed the constraint as the main path rather than an error state, the feature stopped fighting the platform.

24-hour window Template approval Messaging limits
03
In B2B, the structure outlives the screens.

The most consequential decision I made was not a screen. It was refusing to give WhatsApp its own top-level area, because the alternative would have split conversations and context in two. I now start complex B2B work by asking what the objects are and what acts on them, then design screens against that answer.
